Types of Neurons

Neurons come in hundreds of shapes and sizes, and they are sorted two ways: by what they look like and by what they do.

Sorted by shape

Three principal types, each specialized for a particular kind of information processing. In all three the dendrites are the input zone, and in multipolar and bipolar cells the cell body takes synaptic input as well.

Sorted by job

The same three shapes turn up again when neurons are sorted by what they do. This is a simple spinal reflex: a touch on the skin is carried in by a sensory neuron, passed through an interneuron in the spinal cord, and sent out to the muscle by a motor neuron.

Sizes of some neural structures

Each step along this line is ten times the one before it. The whole range, from an ion channel to the largest cell bodies, spans about six orders of magnitude.

Larger structures run off the right end of that line. The cortex is about 3 mm thick, roughly thirty times the width of a large cell body, and an adult human brain is about 15 cm from front to back. In the other direction, a large cell body is about two hundred thousand times wider than the ion channels studded through its membrane.

Where this all came from

An early argument in neuroscience was whether neurons are separate cells at all, or whether the nervous system is one continuous network. Working from the fine detail of individual cells, Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1852 to 1934) showed that neurons come very close together without quite joining. That finding became the neuron doctrine: neurons and the other cells of the brain are structurally, metabolically, and functionally independent, and information passes between them across tiny gaps, which were later named synapses. Cajal worked this out by looking at stained tissue under a microscope and drawing what he saw, decades before anyone could photograph a synapse. Estimates now run to about 1015 synapses in a single human brain.
