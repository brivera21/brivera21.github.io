The Synapse

The same figure can also show where drugs act, since almost every drug that changes behavior does it by interfering with one of these steps.

The cleft is drawn wide enough to see. It is really 20 to 40 nm across.

Where drugs act

A ligand is any substance that binds a receptor. A drug that mimics or strengthens a transmitter is an agonist; one that blocks or weakens it is an antagonist. Press the button above the figure to number these sites on the synapse itself.

Families of neurotransmitters

A chemical counts as a classical transmitter if the presynaptic neuron can make it and store it in the terminal, if it is released when action potentials arrive, if specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane recognize it, if it changes the postsynaptic cell, and if blocking its release stops the presynaptic cell from having its effect. The gas transmitters break several of those rules.

Most synapses in the brain run on amino acids. Glutamate is the most widespread excitatory transmitter and GABA the most widespread inhibitory one. Glutamate's ionotropic AMPA receptors are the most plentiful receptors in the brain; its NMDA receptors matter for memory; its mGluRs are metabotropic and slower. GABAA receptors are ionotropic and let Cl− in, which is why drugs that boost them, such as the benzodiazepines, calm the whole system down. GABAB receptors are metabotropic.

How anyone found this out

Early in the twentieth century the field split into two camps. The sparks held that electricity jumped across the synapse; the soups held that neurons released a chemical. Otto Loewi dreamed the deciding experiment twice. The first night he scribbled notes he could not read the next morning; the second night he went straight to the lab.

He stimulated the vagus nerve of a frog heart, which slowed it, then collected the fluid around that heart and applied it to a second heart. The second heart slowed too. Electricity could not have been carried over in the fluid, so the vagus had to be releasing a chemical. That chemical was later identified as acetylcholine, the first known neurotransmitter, and Loewi got the 1936 Nobel Prize.
