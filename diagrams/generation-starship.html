Generation Starship

How long is the trip, and who arrives?

Travel time uses constant-acceleration kinematics with the same acceleration on the way out and the way in; ship time applies the Lorentz factor to the cruise leg and the exact proper-time integral to the acceleration legs, but the acceleration legs themselves are treated classically, which is accurate to under 1% below 0.2 c. Kinetic energy is the classical ½mv² for the stated dry mass; propellant, shielding, and the energy needed to stop are not included. Generation counts assume that generation length is the same aboard as on Earth.

The tyranny of distance

Light takes 4.24 years to reach Proxima Centauri and 11.9 years to reach Tau Ceti. The fastest object people have ever launched outward, Voyager 1, moves at about 17 km/s, which is 0.0057% of the speed of light. At that speed the Earth-frame travel time to a star at distance D, given by t = D/v, is about 74,000 years for Proxima and 210,000 years for Tau Ceti. Nuclear pulse designs such as Project Orion were sized for a few percent of c, and the British Interplanetary Society's Project Daedalus study (Bond & Martin, 1978) reached 12% of c with a fusion rocket that would still have taken about 50 years to reach Barnard's Star. Even the most generous speed on the slider, 20% of c, puts Tau Ceti roughly 60 years away and TRAPPIST-1 two centuries away.

The acceleration control adds a burn at the start and a matching burn at the end. With constant acceleration a, reaching cruise speed v takes time ta = v/a and covers distance da = v²/(2a); the ship coasts over the remaining D − 2da. If the two burns together would cover more than the whole distance, the ship accelerates to the halfway point and decelerates from there, reaching a peak speed vpeak = √(aD) that is lower than the requested cruise speed. At 1 g the burn to 20% of c lasts 71 days and covers 0.02 light years, so it hardly changes the trip; at 0.01 g the same burn takes 19 years each way, and the crew spends a large part of a Proxima voyage under thrust.

Aboard the ship, clocks run slower by the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). At 20% of c, γ = 1.02, so the crew saves only 2% of the cruise time. The ship-time readout divides the cruise leg by γ and integrates the proper time of the acceleration legs; the effect stays small for every speed on this page, which is the point. Generation ships exist because speeds that make relativity matter are far out of reach.

Energy

The kinetic energy of the ship at cruise is ½mv². Project Daedalus carried a payload near 450 t, and the world-ship studies revisited by Hein et al. (2012) run from a few hundred thousand tonnes to hundreds of millions, so the mass slider spans 10,000 t to 10 million t. A 100,000 t ship at 12% of c carries 6.5 × 1022 J, roughly 600 years of current world electricity generation (about 30,000 TWh per year, or 1.1 × 1020 J). That energy has to be delivered by the propulsion system, and an equal amount has to be removed again to stop, so the readout is a floor rather than a budget. The relativistic kinetic energy (γ − 1)mc² is 3% higher than the classical value at 20% of c.

Who arrives

Fiction has three answers. A relativistic ship, as in Anderson's Tau Zero or Haldeman's The Forever War, lets the crew who launched also land, at the cost of an Earth that has moved on without them. A sleeper ship freezes the crew, which trades the biology of a living society for the biology of suspended animation. A generation ship accepts the travel time and sends a society: the founders launch, their descendants are born and die aboard, and a generation that never chose the voyage arrives. The ladder counts these generations as ⌊ship time / generation length⌋, and the number of dots follows the founding crew size, though a real population would drift up or down over the voyage.

Crew size sets how much genetic diversity the arriving generation inherits. Marin and Beluffi (2018) ran an agent-based simulation of a 6,300-year voyage to Proxima Centauri b and found that a founding crew of about 98 people could survive with strict rules on who may have children and when; below that, small errors in the rules drove the population extinct. A population of about 500 is often cited as the threshold for long-term genetic health. Smith (2014) reviewed the population-genetics literature for Project Hyperion and argued that a 150-year voyage would need 10,000 to 40,000 people to preserve variation against drift, disease, and disaster. The readout states which of these regimes the chosen crew falls in.

The social questions are the ones that fiction keeps returning to. Heinlein's Orphans of the Sky (1963, from two 1941 stories) describes a crew that has forgotten it is on a ship at all and reads the mission plan as scripture. Aldiss's Non-Stop (1958) sends its characters through overgrown corridors toward a truth about the voyage that the ship's society has been built to hide. Robinson's Aurora (2015) treats the ship as a closed ecosystem that decays over 170 years, and asks whether the people born on board owe anything to a plan their great-grandparents made. Hein et al. (2012) put the same questions in engineering language: a world ship must carry a stable population, a closed biosphere, and a political system across a span longer than most nations have existed.

The wait calculation

Kennedy (2006) pointed out a trap. If cruise speed keeps improving at a steady rate, a ship launched later may overtake one launched earlier and arrive first, so there is an incentive to wait. With speed growing as v(t) = v0(1 + r)t, the arrival year is launch year plus D/v(t), and the inset finds the launch year that minimises it. For a voyage that takes T0 years at today's speed and a growth rate k = ln(1 + r), the minimum falls at t* = ln(kT0)/k; at 1% growth a 74,000-year Voyager-speed trip to Proxima is best launched about 660 years from now. Kennedy's argument is that growth cannot continue forever, so at some point the wait stops paying, and that is the moment a generation ship makes sense. The inset caps speed at 50% of c for the same reason.

Real proposals

Project Daedalus (Bond & Martin, 1978) remains the most detailed starship design: a two-stage, 54,000 t fusion rocket burning deuterium and helium-3, unmanned, reaching Barnard's Star in about 50 years without stopping. Breakthrough Starshot, announced in 2016, would use a ground-based laser to push gram-scale probes on light sails to 20% of c, again for flybys, not people. The world-ship studies (Hein et al., 2012) scale Daedalus up to habitats with populations in the thousands and travel times of centuries to millennia, and conclude that the engineering is enormous but not obviously impossible. No crewed interstellar mission has been funded.

Try this

  1. Press Voyager 1 and pick Proxima Centauri. Read the number of generations, then set generation length to 35 and watch it drop by a third.
  2. Set acceleration to 0.01 g and slide the speed up. At the top of the range the burns each take 19 years, and the acceleration-phase readout shows how much of the trip is spent under thrust.
  3. Choose Kepler-452 at 20% of c. The ship time is 2% shorter than the Earth time, which is all relativity offers at this speed.
  4. Push the ship mass to 10 million t at Daedalus speed and compare the kinetic energy with world electricity generation.
  5. Move the founding crew below 98 and read the genetics note; then set growth to 3% per year and watch the best launch year move toward the present.
