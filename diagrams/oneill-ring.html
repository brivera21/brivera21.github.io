Weight from spinning

A ring habitat has no gravity of its own worth mentioning. It makes weight by spinning. Anything that moves in a circle of radius r at angular speed ω is accelerating toward the center at a = ω²r, and the floor of the ring has to push on your feet to supply that acceleration. The push feels exactly like standing on the ground. With ω = 2π · rpm / 60, the Stanford torus (radius 830 m at 1 rpm) gives ω = 0.105 rad/s and a = 9.1 m/s², about 0.93 g; the readouts above use the same two lines for every setting. Rim speed is v = ωr, so the torus rim moves at 87 m/s, and the period is T = 60 / rpm (O'Neill, 1974; Johnson & Holbrow, 1977).

Because a grows with the square of ω but only linearly with r, doubling the radius lets the ring turn 1/√2 as fast for the same weight. Solving ω²r = g for ω gives ω = √(g / r): a 50 m ring needs 4.2 rpm to reach 1 g, the Bernal sphere at 250 m needs 1.9 rpm, and an 8 km diameter Island Three cylinder (Island Three in The High Frontier was drawn at 6.4 km diameter and 32 km long; the 3.2 km radius used here matches its rotation period of roughly 114 s) needs about 0.5 rpm (O'Neill, 1976). Slide the radius up and watch the rotation rate needed for comfort fall. The cost of a slow spin is size, and size is mass, which is the central trade in every design.

How fast is too fast

Spinning too quickly makes people sick, and the reason is the inner ear. When you turn your head inside a rotating room, the two rotations combine and the semicircular canals report a tumble that your eyes do not see. Centrifuge studies from the 1960s onward put most people's tolerance somewhere between 2 and 6 rpm depending on exposure time and training. Hall (2016) reviews these studies and the design guidelines built on them; the common reading is that 2 rpm or less is comfortable for nearly everyone, 2 to 4 rpm needs a period of adaptation, and above 4 rpm many people never adapt. Globus and Hall (2017) push back, arguing that the older experiments used sudden head movements and short exposures, and that 4 rpm, or even higher, is acceptable for a settlement whose residents adapt over days. The disagreement matters for cost. A 1 g habitat at 2 rpm needs a radius of 224 m; at 4 rpm it needs only 56 m, a quarter of the radius and far less structure. Coriolis: the ball that lands to one side

Inside a rotating frame, a moving object seems to be pushed sideways by the Coriolis acceleration, 2ω × v. A ball released from height h falls "down," which is outward toward the rim, and while it falls its sideways speed lags behind the floor that is rushing under it, so it lands a little antispinward of where it was dropped. For a fall time t = √(2h / a), the rotating-frame equations give a deflection of roughly d ≈ (1/3) ω a t³, and the exact answer used for the animation comes from the inertial frame, where the ball moves in a straight line: released at radius r − h, it travels at speed ω(r − h) and reaches the rim after a time set by √(r² − (r − h)²) / (ω(r − h)), while the floor turns through ωt. The difference in angle, times r, is the sideways landing distance. In the Stanford torus that is about 9 cm for a 2 m drop; in a 50 m ring at 4.2 rpm it is about 37 cm, enough to notice when pouring water. The same effect acts on the fluid in your inner ear whenever you nod or turn, which is the physical root of the rpm limits above.

Gravity gradient: heavier feet than head

Since a = ω²r, weight falls in proportion to distance from the axis. Your head, 2 m closer to the axis than your feet, feels a fraction (r − 2) / r of the floor's gravity, so the head-to-foot difference is 2 / r. In the torus that is 0.24 percent, unnoticeable. In a 50 m ring it is 4 percent, and lifting a hand above your head makes it measurably lighter. Toggle "Climb the ladder" to watch apparent gravity drop linearly to zero at the hub, which is why O'Neill placed docking ports and zero-g recreation on the axis (O'Neill, 1976).

What it looks like inside

Standing on the inner surface and looking along the axis, the land curves upward on both sides and, in a full cylinder, continues over your head. There is no horizon in the usual sense. The floor to either side rises to your eye level at a distance r · arccos(1 − 1.6 / r) along the curve: about 52 m in the torus, 13 m in the 50 m ring, and 101 m in Island Three. Beyond that point the ground is above you. The right panel draws this from the true geometry, with the eye-level line dashed. Painters for the 1975 NASA Ames summer study made this view famous, with the land of the torus curving up into haze (Johnson & Holbrow, 1977).

The proposal and its fictions

Gerard O'Neill published the idea in Physics Today in 1974: build habitats in free space from lunar material rather than settling planetary surfaces, spin them for gravity, and light them with mirrors (O'Neill, 1974). The High Frontier (1976) laid out the sequence, Island One (a Bernal sphere), Island Two, and Island Three, a pair of counter-rotating cylinders 32 km long. The 1975 Ames and Stanford summer study produced the Stanford torus, a 10,000-person wheel with a 130 m tube, chosen partly to keep rotation near 1 rpm (Johnson & Holbrow, 1977). Fiction had already been there and kept going. Clarke's Rendezvous with Rama (1973) is a 16 km wide cylinder whose explorers feel weight grow as they descend from the axis, exactly the ladder effect above. Niven's Ringworld (1970) scales the same physics to a ring the size of Earth's orbit; at that radius a 1 g spin has a rim speed of about 1,200 km/s and a period of nine days, and the ring needs an unobtainable material to hold together. The film Elysium (2013) is essentially a Stanford torus, and Cooper Station in Interstellar (2014) is an O'Neill cylinder with baseball played on the curved farmland overhead.
