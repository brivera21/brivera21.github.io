Hard Science Fiction Timeline

Which stories tried hardest to get the science right, and when?

What "hard" means

The phrase "hard science fiction" first appeared in print in a February 1957 book review by P. Schuyler Miller in Astounding Science Fiction (Miller, 1957). It has meant roughly the same thing ever since: fiction that takes known science as a constraint and lets the story grow out of the physical facts rather than around them. Hal Clement described it as a game between writer and reader. The writer tries to break as few laws of nature as possible, and the reader tries to catch the slips. His essay "Whirligig world" showed the working behind Mission of Gravity, down to the calculation of how gravity varies across a fast-spinning oblate planet (Clement, 1953).

Fans later borrowed the idea of a hardness scale from mineralogy. The "Mohs scale of science fiction hardness" is a heuristic, not a measurement, but it captures the useful point that hardness is a spectrum: one invented exception (a wormhole, a faster-than-light drive) followed rigorously is different from a story where anything goes. Westfahl (1996) and the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction entry by Nicholls and Langford (2023) both stress that the label describes an attitude toward science more than any fixed set of topics.

How the timeline follows real science

The fiction clusters around real advances. Verne wrote before rocketry existed and reached for a cannon. Tsiolkovsky derived the rocket equation in 1903 and then wrote a novel to show what it implied. Once Goddard flew a liquid-fuel rocket in 1926, and especially after Sputnik in 1957 and Apollo 11 in 1969, spaceflight stories could draw on engineering that existed. O'Neill's 1974 habitat proposal and the 1978 Project Daedalus report fed a wave of megastructure and starship fiction, from Rendezvous with Rama to Eon. The discovery of 51 Pegasi b in 1995 gave later interstellar stories real targets, and Aurora and Project Hail Mary both aim at Tau Ceti. Computing and neuroscience open a third thread in the 1990s and 2000s, with Egan's uploaded minds and Watts's argument about consciousness.

Several of these works connect to the other two pages in this set. Spin gravity in 2001, Rama, Ringworld and Interstellar is the physics on the O'Neill ring page. Orphans of the Sky, Non-Stop, Aurora and the slower-than-light ships of Vinge and Reynolds are the subject of the generation starship page.

A spectrum, not a canon

The forty works here were chosen because each takes at least one area of science seriously, and because together they show how the field moved. Many equally careful works are missing, and some entries include a deliberate exception (Weir's opening dust storm, Sagan's wormhole) that the author acknowledged. Treat the list as a place to start reading, and treat the domain colors as the main thing each work leans on rather than the only thing it gets right.
